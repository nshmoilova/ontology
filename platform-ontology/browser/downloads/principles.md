# Principles at a glance

*What a principle is, how one is derived, what each one means, and which candidates were evaluated and why they stand where they do*

**Audience.** Anyone reading or proposing a change to the ontology — architects, reviewers, and agents checking a proposal against the rules.

A decision picks among alternatives once. A principle constrains every future pick. This ontology keeps twenty, each stated once, each with a test a proposal is checked against, and each tied to the invariants that enforce it. Nothing becomes a principle by being asserted: a candidate is evaluated against four fitness criteria, and most candidates end up recorded as decisions, out of scope, or not yet decided.

## 1. What a principle is

Three kinds of statement live in this repository and they are kept apart. A definition says what a term denotes. A decision records one choice among alternatives at one point in time, with what was rejected and why. A principle is a rule the ontology obeys: it constrains every future decision, it can be phrased as a test, and something enforces it. The layer exists because one rule, 'the token is evidence, the grant is authority', had been restated in five places and was drifting into five versions. Now each rule is stated once, in principles.json, and referenced everywhere else.

**Principles:** [P4 — The token is evidence; the grant is authority](https://nshmoilova.github.io/ontology/#/principles#P4)  
**Decisions:** [D22 — Grants are projections, never hand-authored](https://nshmoilova.github.io/ontology/#/decisions#D22), [D24 — The ingress token contract](https://nshmoilova.github.io/ontology/#/decisions#D24), [D44 — Sessions are sender-constrained, DPoP-style](https://nshmoilova.github.io/ontology/#/decisions#D44)

## 2. How a principle is derived

A candidate sentence is evaluated, never simply added. It passes only if it meets all four criteria: generative — it has already produced more than one decision or invariant; testable — it can be asked as a question of a new proposal; a commitment, not a choice — it constrains all future selections rather than making one; enforced or enforceable — an invariant, build check or CI gate realises it. Candidates that fail are recorded with the reason so the evaluation is not repeated — kept as decisions, out of scope, or terms-first; a few were later promoted once the terms existed, and the record says so. Each row below shows the principle's live status: confirmed once a decision was made because of it, provisional until then. A promoted principle carries its scope (contract, CI, runtime-asserted, review-time), any open exception with an owner and a backlog address, and a status: provisional until some decision is made because of it, confirmed after. Evaluating a candidate has repeatedly found gaps — an undelivered invariant, an optional actor on a decision, a self-approved membership in the seed data — which is the strongest evidence a rule is real.

| Criterion | The question asked |
| --- | --- |
| Generative | Has it already produced more than one decision or invariant? A rule applied once is a decision; a principle keeps producing them. |
| Testable | Can it be turned into a question a reviewer asks of every new proposal, with a yes or no answer? If it cannot, it is a slogan. |
| Commitment | Does it constrain every future choice, or make one? |
| Enforced | Which shape, gate or check rejects a violation? If none, can one be written? |

## 3. Platform principles — what the platform obeys

These constrain the platform's behaviour and are enforced by SHACL shapes over platform data. Where the runtime half cannot be seen in the graph, the principle says so and claims only the contract half.

| Principle | In short | Example from the model | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 The boundary, not the resource | The platform decides whether a request may enter an application at a deployment: tenant, environment, region, channel. What it may do inside is the application's decision, never the platform's. | Ingress asks 'may this tenant's subject enter digital-bank at this endpoint in prod-US', never 'may they view account 42'. | Keeps the platform ignorant of application internals, so an application can be replaced by an opaque identifier and every platform check still works. |
| P2 Governance defines; enforcement actuates | Whatever defines desired state never enforces it, and whatever enforces never defines. | The control plane declares that a route binds tenant T; ingress enforces it and cannot change it. | A component that both decides and enforces its own configuration is the loop an auditor looks for first. |
| P3 Agents execute; humans authorize | Any automation may act — a scheduled job, a service, an AI agent — whether the platform, a tenant or an external legal entity runs it. Every grant of authority carries a human approval by a member of the party whose authority it is: the tenant's for tenant authority, the platform's for platform authority. Revocation is exempt. | A provisioning agent run by a third party executes a data-plane change under a declaration a platform member approved: allowed. The same agent's admission to a tenant approved by its operator's staff: rejected — they are members of no authorizing party. Withdrawing the agent's access needs no approval at all. | Automation scales action; accountability must not scale away with it — least of all when the automation is somebody else's. Operating an agent and authorizing it are two accountabilities, and the second never follows from the first. |
| P4 The token is evidence; the grant is authority | A token proves who the caller is. It never says what they may do. What they may do is decided by grants, which can be withdrawn at any time without touching the token. | Alice's token proves it is Alice. Her grants decide what she can actually do. Take her grants away and her next request is denied, even though her token is still valid. | Claims freeze at issuance; entitlements must not. |
| P12 One path, no bypass | There is one way to reach anything the platform governs, and it is the governed way. Reaching it any other way is a violation, not an exception. | A request reaches an application only through a route with an enforcement point in front of it. A change reaches a data plane only through a declaration. A grant exists only because governance produced it. Anything arriving without that trail is rejected. | Alternate paths are where controls are quietly bypassed. |
| P13 Decisions are policy-based and externalized | Every decision evaluates a named policy at a named decision point; the enforcement point is a different component. | A decision names its policy, its action, its outcome and its PDP; a node may not be both PDP and PEP. | Decisions embedded in code cannot be audited, changed or reasoned about. |
| P15 Trust boundaries fail closed | If the platform cannot tell whether a request should be allowed, it says no. Missing or out-of-date information never quietly turns into a yes. | A request arrives and something needed to check it is missing, stale, or the check itself cannot complete. The request is refused and the gap is reported, rather than let through on the assumption it was probably fine. | Security is rarely lost by decision. It is lost when something breaks and traffic keeps flowing. Failing closed turns a broken control into a visible outage instead of a silent hole. |
| P16 Isolation boundaries are explicit and enforced | Every isolation boundary is stated and every crossing rejected; a declaration's reach is bounded by scope. | A route serving another tenant's application is a cross-tenant endpoint; a cell-per-tenant data plane serving two tenants is a violation. | Blast radius is a design property only if the model can compute it (CQ-8). |
| P17 Every action is traceable | Every request and security-relevant change leaves evidence tracing to the responsible principal — who acted and who authorized. | A policy change names its executor and its human approver; a decision names its requesting principal; a record whose actor is optional is not evidence. | 'Who did this, and who let them' must be answerable after the fact, hop by hop. |
| P18 Intent precedes state | Nothing exists without a reason upstream. It goes no further than the reason, and ends when the reason ends. | A tenant subscribes to a capability. The platform provisions it for that tenant, only where the subscription covers, and tears it down when the subscription ends. | Every access has a reason, stays inside it, and does not outlive it. That rules out standing access nobody can justify. |
| P19 No one approves their own act | The executor of a change is never its approver; the principal an admission grants access to never approves it. | alice may not approve her own tenant membership; an agent's change is approved by someone other than the agent. | Separation of duties is the control auditors test first, and it was one shape away from enforced. |
| P20 Consumers depend on contracts, never on implementations | A capability is consumed through its published contract; a consumer depends on a contract version, never on what realises the capability; a contract changes only through a published, approved version. | An application's requirement pins version 1.2.0 of the managed-database contract; a version published without a change declaration, or a consumer left on a retired version, is a violation. | Consumers survive re-implementation; a change to an interface has an approver and a history. |
| P21 Data does not cross a jurisdiction without a recorded basis | A tenant's data of each category has jurisdictions it must stay in and may sit in; storing, operating or admitting access to it from any other is a crossing, allowed only under a transfer permission with a basis. Jurisdiction is law, not location. | A tenant's customer data in a US region while its residency requires the EU, a plane operated by an entity under another jurisdiction, or an admission resolved from one — each a violation unless a permission with a basis exists. | Residency and sovereignty become facts the graph defends at provisioning and admission, rather than clauses in a contract nobody can run. |

## 4. Modelling principles — the form of the ontology

These constrain how the model is built and are enforced by OWL axioms and build checks over the ontology itself.

| Principle | In short | Example from the model | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P5 An identifier resolves to an entity; it does not substitute for one | A name in a namespace points at a thing; it is never used in place of the thing. | A domain resolves to a tenant through a governed route binding; the domain is not the tenant. A client_id identifies a registration, nothing more. | Substituting identifiers for entities is how tenancy, sessions and callers get confused with their labels. |
| P6 OWL for semantics, SHACL for contracts | OWL defines what a term means. SHACL decides what data is accepted. The two are never mixed: a definition never doubles as a validation rule. | OWL says a session is bound to a principal; SHACL says every session must have exactly one expiry. | Meaning and admission rules change at different speeds and answer to different reviewers. |
| P7 Vocabularies are closed schemes of individuals | A fixed set of values is a SKOS scheme, and membership in it is machine-checked. | Decision outcomes are exactly Permit, Deny, Indeterminate, NotApplicable; a fifth is a violation. | Open string vocabularies drift; closed schemes make 'deliberately small and stable' checkable. |
| P8 Specifications first, never products | Align to open specifications, then recorded platform decisions, then custom terms; name no products; record every departure. | RFC 9493 subject identifiers and DPoP are named and declared on their terms; an IdP vendor is not. Keeping the session id out of the token is a recorded deviation (D56). | A vocabulary that encodes today's implementation ages badly. |
| P14 Principals are modelled uniformly | Humans, services and agents share one identity and authorization model; they differ by attributes and permitted flows. | Human and workload are disjoint subclasses of one Principal class; every binding types to the common class; only flows differ, such as approval requiring a human. | Parallel mechanisms per kind of caller are where one kind escapes the controls. |
